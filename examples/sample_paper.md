# Brief Gratitude Journaling Reduces Exam Stress: A Randomized Experiment

R. Veldmann and T. Okonjo-Hart, Department of Psychology, University of Norhaven

## Abstract

Exam periods are a major source of stress for students. We tested whether a
one-week gratitude journaling exercise reduces perceived exam stress. A total
of 68 undergraduate students were randomly assigned to a gratitude journaling
condition or a neutral writing control condition. Participants in the
journaling condition reported lower exam stress than controls, and the effect
was still present at a two-week follow-up. Brief gratitude interventions may
be a cheap and scalable way to support students.

## Introduction

Gratitude interventions have been linked to higher well-being and lower
negative affect, but evidence from student samples during exam periods is
scarce. Earlier studies relied on colossal information from wellness apps and
could not establish causality. We therefore ran a preregistered randomized
experiment. We hypothesized that participants who kept a gratitude journal for
one week would report lower exam stress than participants in a control
condition (H1), and that trait gratitude would be negatively related to exam
stress (H2).

## Method

### Participants

We recruited 63 undergraduate students from an introductory psychology course
in exchange for course credit. Three participants were excluded because they
failed both attention checks, leaving a final sample of 60 participants. Of
these, 41 of 60 (68%) identified as women. Participants were randomly assigned
to the gratitude journaling condition (n = 32) or the control condition
(n = 28).

### Procedure and measures

For seven days, participants in the journaling condition listed three things
they were grateful for each evening; controls listed three things they had
done that day. On day 8, all participants rated their exam stress on a single
item ("How stressed do you feel about your upcoming exams?") from 1 (not at
all) to 7 (extremely). As a manipulation check, they rated how grateful they
had felt during the week on the same 7-point format. Trait gratitude was
measured at baseline with a single 7-point item.

## Results

### Manipulation check

Participants in the journaling condition (n = 32) reported a mean gratitude of
5.25 (SD = 0.98), compared with a mean of 4.50 (SD = 0.92) in the control
condition (n = 28), t(58) = 3.12, p = .003.

### Exam stress

Supporting H1, the journaling group (n = 32) reported a mean exam stress of
4.53 (SD = 1.02), whereas the control group (n = 28) reported a mean exam
stress of 5.19 (SD = 1.08), t(58) = 2.41, p = .019. In addition, 23 of 60
participants (45%) scored above the scale midpoint on exam stress. Supporting
H2, trait gratitude was negatively related to exam stress, r(58) = -.31,
p = .016. The groups did not differ in baseline trait gratitude,
t(58) = 0.62, p = .538.

### Follow-up

Forty participants completed the two-week follow-up. The journaling group
still reported significantly lower exam stress than the control group,
t(38) = 1.80, p = .02.

## Discussion

A one-week gratitude journal significantly reduced exam stress, and the
benefit persisted two weeks later. Limitations include the single-item outcome
and the modest sample from one university. Future work should test longer
interventions and objective outcomes such as grades.
